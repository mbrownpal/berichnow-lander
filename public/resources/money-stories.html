Before You Begin

You already know most of
what is in here.

You have made money. You have probably made a lot of it, and somewhere along the way you noticed that the making did not deliver what it promised. You read the book, so you already know the mechanism. Your relationship with money was installed before you had any say in the matter, and it has been quietly running your decisions ever since.

What follows is the excavation. Every question in here has been asked in a room with high earners who paid real money to sit in it, and every one of them has produced the same thing: a moment where someone hears themselves say a sentence out loud and realizes they have been carrying it since they were seven years old.

You cannot get that moment by reading. You get it by writing.

The only rule

Tell the truth. Nobody is going to read this. The value of every page in your hands is set entirely by how honest you are willing to be on it.

The Money Stories Workbook1
How To Use This

Six instructions,
and then you are on your own.

01

Write by hand.

Print this and use a pen. Handwriting is slower than your defenses, which is the entire point. Typing lets you edit while you think, and editing while you think is how the story survives another decade.

02

Set a timer for ten minutes per question.

Keep the pen moving for the full ten even when you believe you are finished at four. The first answer is the one you have told before. The answer that changes something usually arrives after the first one runs out.

03

Do not do this all at once.

Five parts, five sittings, one per day or one per week. Your nervous system needs time between excavations, and sleep does more of this work than you do.

04

Separate knowing from action.

You can admit something is true without deciding what to do about it. Most people refuse the truth because they assume admitting it obligates them to act by Friday. Naming it is the whole assignment for now.

05

Go for the minimum effective dose.

The examination can become its own sophisticated avoidance. Do enough work to see the pattern, interrupt it, and then go live your life.

06

Expect to be unimpressed with yourself.

Every story in here served you at some point. Your brain built it to keep you safe and it did the job. You are updating software that shipped a long time ago, so treat it the way you would treat any old system that got you this far.

One

What You Inherited

Awareness

Your childhood brain was not equipped to parse what was happening around it, so it did what brains do and made meaning out of the available evidence. Those conclusions were never rational, which is exactly why they sit upstream of your behavior today. Before you can interrupt anything, you have to find out what got installed.

Exercise One

The Money Autobiography

Twelve questions. Ten minutes each. Somewhere in a quiet room with the phone in another one, and instrumental music if the monkey mind needs something to chew on.

Notice the language in these questions, because it was chosen carefully. The word witness appears more than once, and it is doing specific work. Parents often say one thing about money and do another, and what shaped you was what you saw rather than what you were told. Go for the lived experience underneath the family narrative.

You may hit resistance when the questions turn toward your parents. When we are young our parents function as our gods, and our survival depends on staying in their good graces, so the instinct to protect them is old and load-bearing. You can hold both truths at once. They were doing the best they could with what they had, and some of what they did shaped you in ways that no longer serve you. Nothing in this exercise requires you to blame anyone.

If you get stuck

Write the sentence "I don't know" over and over until something else arrives. It always does, usually within about ninety seconds.

Exercise Three

The Vow

When I was in elementary school my parents fought every night, and they only ever fought about one thing. Watching the two people I loved most tear each other apart over money, I made a promise to myself that I would earn so much as an adult that I would never have to worry about it.

That vow built my career, and it delivered exactly what I asked it for. The second half of the promise is what nearly cost me everything, because my subconscious had quietly equated being responsible with money to worrying about money, and I had sworn never to worry again.

Most people carry a vow like this. It usually forms in a single moment, in the voice of a child, and it is almost always a promise about what will never happen again.

Find the moment. What did you promise yourself, and how old were you?
Write the vow as a single sentence, in the words the child used.
Where has this vow served you, and where has it started charging rent?

Two

The Ten Stories

Insight

After several years of retreats and a few hundred long conversations with high earners, the same stories kept walking through the door wearing different clothes. The details vary enormously and the structure almost never does. At least one of these will feel uncomfortably familiar, and most people are running three or four at once.

How To Read These

Look for the behavior,
not the sentence.

Almost nobody recognizes their own story by its name, because from inside a story the behavior it produces feels like ordinary good judgment. What you can spot is the behavior. Read the middle section of each card first, and if something in it describes a Tuesday you actually had, keep reading.

These exist on a spectrum, and none of them is inherently a defect. Scarcity gets treated as the villain and abundance as the cure, which is a marketing story rather than a true one. I was enormously abundant right up until the point I nearly ran out of money, and a little more scarcity would have served me well. Neutrality is the target, because a neutral mind can actually read the data in front of it.

Score each story from zero to five as you go. Zero means the description does nothing to you. Five means you had to put the workbook down.

Story 01

“Rich People Are Evil”

Money corrupts, and anyone holding a lot of it took it from someone who needed it more.
How it shows up

You empty the account almost as fast as it fills. You undercharge and call it fairness. You downplay a good year to your family, drive something deliberately unremarkable, and feel a low hum of apology whenever the subject of your success comes up in your hometown.

Where it usually comes from

A parent who railed against the wealthy at the dinner table, a religious teaching absorbed young, or a community where standing out was punished more reliably than failing.

Offense and defense

It keeps you generous and it keeps you honest. It also guarantees that you will spend a lifetime building things you are not permitted to keep.

Three

The Winds Beneath

The Four Drives

Your stories sit on top of something older. Twenty-five centuries ago the Buddha described four pairs of opposites that blow human beings around, and modern behavioral economics arrived at nearly the same list and called them cognitive biases. Every story you just scored is a narrative layer sitting on one of these.

01

Pleasure and Pain

Seeking comfort and avoiding discomfort is the most primal drive you have, and it predates society by a few hundred thousand years. It is the root of reactive behavior.

How it spends

It buys the upgrade at the moment of stress. It avoids the conversation with the accountant. It orders the thing that will arrive tomorrow because tomorrow is sooner than the feeling will pass.

Where in your life are you spending money, or avoiding a decision, in order to feel better right now?
The Four Drives33
02

Gain and Loss

Losing something registers roughly twice as intensely as gaining the same thing, which means your nervous system is systematically miscalibrated toward protection.

How it spends

It holds a position too long because selling makes the loss real. It refuses a good opportunity because the downside is vivid and the upside is theoretical. It counts what it might lose more carefully than what it might build.

Where is fear of losing something driving a decision that the numbers do not support?
The Four Drives34
03

Praise and Blame

The desire to be well regarded by the people in your immediate circle is old, deep, and almost entirely invisible while it is happening. This one is mine.

How it spends

It takes the meeting because someone impressive asked. It picks up the tab. It says yes to the investment because of how it will feel to be introduced as the investor at the restaurant.

Where are you spending money, or time, in exchange for someone's approval?
The Four Drives35
04

Fame and Disgrace

The version most people miss, because the word fame calls celebrity to mind. The useful reframe is the curation of your image for people you will never meet.

How it spends

It optimizes purchases for how they will read to strangers. It treats every choice as a public relations decision. Frugality performs here just as loudly as extravagance, because the ten-year-old sedan is also sending a message.

Where are you managing how you are perceived by people whose opinion cannot actually affect your life?

Pretending Not To Know

Radical Truth Telling

Stories and patterns explain how you got here and why you behave the way you do. They cannot account for what you are actively refusing to look at, because the lie sits upstream of the pattern. If you are deceiving yourself, no amount of insight into your programming will surface anything true.

The Question

What am I
pretending
not to know?

This question bypasses the defenses you have spent a lifetime building. Most people can answer it in under ten seconds and then spend twenty minutes explaining why the answer does not count.

We say we want clarity, and most of us do not, because clarity forces a choice. Pretending not to know is how you stay safe from the perceived pain of change while telling yourself you are simply confused about what to do.

Sit with it for ten full minutes. Write whatever arrives, including the answers you intend to cross out.

Exercise Seven

The Rewrite

Internal dissonance is the tension of being divided against yourself, and it shows up as anxiety, procrastination, perfectionism, and a background hum of stress that never quite resolves. It hides inside sentences that contain just enough truth to pass inspection.

Consider a statement most people would accept without a second look: I can't quit this job I hate because I need the money. The second half is true and the first half gives away your agency, which makes the whole thing a comfortable lie. Quitting requires reducing expenses or tolerating uncertainty, and what you are actually saying is that you are unwilling to do either. Both of those may be entirely reasonable positions.

Written honestly it becomes: I hate my job, and I am choosing to stay because I do not want to give up my lifestyle or sit in the uncertainty of finding another one. Nothing about your circumstances changed. You simply took back the agency, and now staying is a choice rather than a sentence.

The comfortable version
Start with "I can't" or "I have to"
The honest version
Start with "I am choosing"

Where you perform

External dissonance is the friction between your inner life and the one you are actually living in front of other people. You betray something you know to be true in order to manufacture harmony, acceptance, or belonging, and you do it so smoothly that it barely registers as a decision.

Two truths make this one easier to dismantle. Nobody thinks about you nearly as much as you do, because they are fully occupied worrying that everyone can see them failing. And nobody can read your mind, which means every secret test you administer is a test you designed to be failed.

Five

Running The Loop

Action and Integration

Everything to this point has been excavation, and excavation on its own is self-indulgence. Insight that does not become action is a hobby. What follows is the process I adapted from the OODA loop I learned in the cockpit and have now run on myself and a few hundred clients for the better part of a decade.

The Model

The Alignment Loop

1
Awareness

Witness the thought, the feeling, or the behavior without doing anything about it. Notice that your reaction is larger than the situation calls for.

2
Insight

Ask where in your past this pattern comes from. It is easy to get stuck here, because mining your history feels like progress while producing none.

3
Action

Interrupt the pattern with a mechanical fix, an emotional fix, or ideally both. This stage is the one that matters most and the one most people skip.

4
Integration

Repeat the new action until your body believes it. Then keep watching, because the pattern will return in a subtler costume.

The model is a loop rather than a line for a reason. You will never eliminate these patterns entirely, and the goal was never elimination. You are working toward being at choice about when to run them, which is a very different and much more achievable target.

The Interrupt
Breathe, three times

Exhale everything, drawing navel to spine. Breathe into the belly, take a second sip of air into the chest, and pause. Exhale from the belly first and rest for a few heartbeats at the bottom.

What Happens Now

The loop is an infinite game.

You have run this once, on one story, and the process does not change when you scale it. The same four stages that worked on the way you handle money will work on the way you handle your health, your calendar, your marriage, and your reaction to being stuck in traffic. Money was only ever the access point, chosen because it is the domain where the evidence is easiest to audit.

Finite games have fixed rules and produce winners. Infinite games never end and only become more refined. Your life will keep handing you the exact assignments you need, arriving as a failed deal, a hard conversation, or an unreasonable flash of anger in an ordinary moment.
